# EVER Website Vision

**Brand:** EVER — East Valley Electronic Recycle  
**Purpose:** Public website design and interaction direction  
**Status:** Foundational vision; copy, service claims, fees, hours, locations, and certifications must be verified before launch  
**Created:** August 15, 2026

## The idea in one sentence

EVER should feel like a modern technology-recovery operation that gives residents an immediate answer and gives businesses a clear, credible path from retired equipment to pickup, inventory, recovery, reuse, and responsible recycling.

The site is not a digital brochure with animation added on top. It is the front door to the operation.

## What carries forward from the Homeful Pro philosophy

The transferable lesson is not Homeful Pro's visual treatment. It is its discipline:

- Build the page as a sequence of deliberate scenes, not a stack of interchangeable marketing sections.
- Let interaction demonstrate what the company does.
- Use motion to explain a transition, confirm an action, or advance the story.
- Give each section one job and a distinct spatial composition.
- Use large, confident type, meaningful negative space, and plain language.
- Make the experience feel alive without keeping the screen in constant motion.
- Remove labels, effects, badges, and containers that do not help someone understand or complete something.

EVER should interpret those principles through a clean industrial-technology identity: bright, exact, useful, local, and operational. It should not resemble Homeful with different colors.

## Strategic position

EVER should not lead with abstract environmental virtue. People arrive with a practical problem:

- A resident wants to know whether an item is accepted, whether it costs anything, and where to take it.
- A business needs old technology removed, documented, secured, recovered, reused, or recycled without creating another operational headache.

The public promise is therefore:

> Old technology, handled clearly.

Environmental benefit is an important outcome, but convenience, security, recovery, and competence earn attention first.

EVER should be presented as more than a commodity recycler. The expandable service story is:

- **Receive** — convenient drop-off, scheduled pickup, or collection events.
- **Identify** — understand the equipment, quantity, condition, and data-bearing assets.
- **Recover** — find remaining equipment, component, or resale value where appropriate.
- **Reuse** — keep viable technology in use when a responsible path exists.
- **Recycle** — process end-of-life material through appropriate recycling channels.
- **Secure** — apply defined handling and data processes without making unverified claims.

This framing lets the company grow into IT asset disposition, equipment resale, data services, office cleanouts, recurring pickups, buybacks, and reporting without outgrowing the brand.

## Desired feeling

The website should feel:

- Premium, but not luxurious
- Technical, but not cold
- Industrial, but not dirty or heavy
- Local, but not small-time
- Environmentally responsible, but not covered in green recycling clichés
- Interactive, but never restless
- Confident enough to be simple

The reference point is the clarity of a high-end product company combined with the credibility of a real logistics operation.

## Brand expression

### Color

The site remains predominantly white. Deep navy carries headlines, body copy, navigation, and serious business sections. The established blue-to-cyan-to-lime treatment in the EVER `V` is the signature energy source.

Use the accent colors by role:

- **Blue:** action, routing, links, selected states
- **Cyan:** information, movement, scanning, active transitions
- **Lime:** successful acceptance, recovered value, completion
- **Navy:** authority, commercial depth, data security, contrast scenes
- **White:** the primary canvas and source of restraint

Do not spread all accent colors evenly through every section. A scene should usually have one dominant accent. Sample production values from the approved logo assets rather than inventing near-matches.

The logo stays flat and clean. Do not add glow, bevel, shadow, or animated distortion to it. If the `V` contains the approved color progression, preserve it exactly.

### Typography

Use a bold modern grotesk or geometric sans with excellent numerals and highly readable lowercase forms. Headlines can be compressed through scale and tight line-height, not through an overly condensed novelty font. Body copy should feel calm and operational.

Recommended hierarchy:

- Hero display: bold, compact, approximately 64–104px on large screens
- Section statements: approximately 44–72px
- Body: approximately 18–20px
- Controls: 16–18px, never miniature
- Supporting labels: sentence case wherever possible

Avoid making every label uppercase. Reserve uppercase for short, structural phrases such as the main hero statement or a truck-like service line.

### Image direction

Use original photography as soon as operations exist:

- Clean tables of real laptops, servers, networking equipment, and components
- Hands scanning labels, sorting equipment, or preparing a pickup
- Organized bins, pallets, carts, and vehicles
- Business pickups in recognizable East Valley environments
- Close details such as ports, asset tags, circuit boards, drive trays, and cable geometry
- Real team members and the actual facility

Photography should show order being created from complexity. Avoid landfill mountains, hands holding a seedling over a motherboard, glowing green globes, generic server-room stock images, and staged people pointing at clipboards.

The circuit-line motif may appear as a quiet routing device or texture, but it should never become wallpaper.

## Homepage narrative

The homepage is a guided decision, not a catalog. It should unfold in the following scenes.

### Scene 1 — Hero: one problem, two paths

**Headline:**

> OLD TECH?  
> WE'LL TAKE IT.

**Support:**

> Free local electronics drop-off and commercial technology recovery across Arizona's East Valley.

**Primary choices:**

- `I have a few items` — enters the residential answer flow
- `I represent a business` — enters the commercial pickup/ITAD flow

The labels should describe the visitor, not force them to understand industry terminology.

Small factual trust line beneath the actions:

> East Valley, Arizona · Most electronics accepted free · Veteran owned & operated

Only publish “free,” location, coverage, or acceptance language after the operating policy is final.

#### Visual concept: the V as a routing junction

The approved EVER wordmark anchors the upper-left navigation. A large cropped form derived from the `V` sits behind or beside the hero copy—not as a decorative gradient blob, but as a visual fork between the two customer paths.

On first load, a single cyan route travels into the junction and separates once toward **Drop-off** and once toward **Business pickup**. The path animation runs once, in roughly 900–1200ms, and settles completely. Hovering or focusing a choice highlights only its route. Selecting it moves the highlighted route into the next scene.

On desktop, the composition is asymmetric: editorial copy occupies roughly five columns, while the routing junction and the first useful answer occupy the remaining space. On mobile, the junction becomes a compact divider between two full-width actions; it never sits behind text.

This gives EVER a recognizable hero behavior without a looping background, particle field, autoplay video, or fake equipment dashboard.

### Scene 2 — Residential quick answer: “Can I bring this?”

This is a functional tool and should appear immediately after the residential choice or be reachable from the primary navigation.

**Prompt:**

> What are you recycling?

Use large, searchable categories with simple line illustrations or tightly cropped real equipment photography:

- Computers
- Laptops
- Phones and tablets
- Monitors
- TVs
- Servers and networking
- Printers
- Gaming systems
- Cables and accessories
- Batteries or other special-handling categories only if policy supports them

When an item is chosen, the interface should replace uncertainty with a plain result:

- **Accepted — no recycling fee**
- **Accepted — fee applies**
- **Contact us first**
- **Not currently accepted**

Each result must include the next useful information: conditions, preparation guidance, fee if applicable, location, open hours, and directions. Do not communicate state by green or red alone.

The visitor should not have to request a quote, create an account, or complete a lead form to learn whether a household item is accepted.

#### Interaction

The selected item visually moves into a clean “accepted route” beneath the selector. A short line advances from the item to the result, followed by a restrained check or information symbol. This takes 250–400ms and occurs only after selection. Changing the item morphs the existing result rather than stacking new cards.

### Scene 3 — Commercial shift: old technology still has value

The page changes from bright white to a deep navy field, signaling a more capable commercial mode without becoming a separate brand.

**Headline:**

> YOUR OLD TECHNOLOGY  
> STILL HAS A PATH FORWARD.

**Support:**

> EVER handles retired electronics from pickup through identification, recovery, reuse, and recycling.

The business story should emphasize reduced workload, data-aware handling, clear communication, and potential value recovery. Never promise resale value, security outcomes, certification, or destruction methods that are not operationally supported.

#### Interactive pickup brief

Instead of a generic “Get a quote” form, let a business build a useful pickup brief in three short steps:

1. **What do you have?** Computers, laptops, servers, networking, phones/tablets, monitors, printers, mixed equipment, or office cleanout.
2. **How much?** 1–10, 11–50, 51–250, 250+, or “I have an inventory list.”
3. **What matters most?** Pickup, inventory, data handling, recycling, recovery value, recurring service, or full cleanout.

The right side becomes a live plain-language summary, for example:

> Mixed office equipment · approximately 51–250 items · pickup and inventory requested

Only after the visitor sees a useful summary should the site ask for company and contact information. Allow a spreadsheet or equipment list upload at the appropriate step, with clear file limits and privacy guidance.

The commercial interaction should feel like preparing a work order, not chatting with a bot.

### Scene 4 — The EVER route

**Statement:**

> WE DON'T TREAT USABLE TECHNOLOGY LIKE TRASH.

Show one continuous material route rather than five feature cards:

`Receive → Identify → Recover → Reuse → Recycle`

On desktop, a physical object or abstract equipment silhouette moves only as the user progresses through the section. Each stage reveals one sentence and one authentic operational detail. The path may branch at **Identify** to show that not every asset has the same outcome.

On mobile, this becomes a vertical sequence with a persistent line and native scrolling. Do not pin the viewport for a long forced animation.

The point is to explain judgment and process: technology is evaluated before its next path is chosen.

### Scene 5 — Value recovery

Use a bright editorial composition with a close photograph of high-value business equipment crossing the grid.

**Headline:**

> SOME OLD ELECTRONICS AREN'T WASTE.

Explain that eligible servers, laptops, networking equipment, and components may retain value. Use “may,” “eligible,” and “evaluated”; do not imply every pickup produces a payout.

The interaction can be a simple comparison scrubber between **Recycle everything** and **Evaluate first**. It should reveal possible pathways—reuse, component recovery, resale, or material recycling—not fictional dollar amounts. Avoid calculators until EVER has reliable operating data and terms.

### Scene 6 — Local service, made concrete

Use an abstract East Valley route map built from the same circuit/routing language, with real city names rather than a generic glowing map:

- Gilbert
- Mesa
- Chandler
- Queen Creek
- Tempe
- San Tan Valley
- Other verified service areas

The map is not a fake live-tracking interface. Locations illuminate as they enter view; there are no moving vehicles, pulsing GPS dots, or invented arrival times.

Residential visitors see the drop-off destination, hours, preparation details, and directions. Business visitors see pickup coverage and the relevant service-area note. Search-focused city pages should answer local questions honestly and lead back into the same tools rather than repeat keyword-heavy boilerplate.

### Scene 7 — Trust without theater

Trust should be specific and selectively placed:

- Veteran owned & operated
- Actual accepted-material and data-handling policies
- Real certifications only after they are active
- Real reviews after they exist
- Actual insurance or downstream information when appropriate
- Clear explanation of what happens after a pickup request

Use the veteran-owned badge once near the hero trust line and once in the deeper trust/footer area. It is a supporting credential, not part of the primary wordmark and not a repeated patriotic motif.

Do not use an official certification seal unless EVER has the certification and permission to display it. Do not use placeholder statistics or animated counters.

### Scene 8 — Final decision

End with a large, quiet choice:

> HAVE ELECTRONICS?  
> WE'LL TELL YOU THE NEXT STEP.

- `Check a drop-off item`
- `Plan a business pickup`

The visual route from the hero returns here, now complete. This is the only repeated large motif and gives the page a deliberate ending.

## Site architecture

### Primary navigation

- Recycle
- Business
- What We Accept
- How It Works
- About EVER
- Resources
- `Plan a Pickup` as the persistent business action

Keep navigation restrained. “Residential” and “Commercial” can be used in internal structure, but the visitor-facing actions should use clearer language: **Drop off electronics** and **Plan a business pickup**.

### Core routes

#### `/`

The dual-path homepage and brand story.

#### `/recycle`

Residential decision page. Lead with the acceptance finder, then fees, preparation, location, hours, directions, and frequently asked questions.

#### `/what-we-accept`

A searchable, filterable source of truth—not a decorative gallery. Every category has status, conditions, possible fee, and preparation details.

#### `/business`

Commercial overview covering pickup, ITAD direction, inventory, recovery, reuse/recycling, recurring service, office cleanouts, and collection events as supported.

#### `/business/pickup`

The focused pickup brief and submission flow. Preserve entered answers when moving between steps and make confirmation expectations explicit.

#### `/how-it-works`

The operational route from receipt through final disposition. Use photography and plain-language process explanations rather than a sustainability manifesto.

#### `/about`

The real founding story, East Valley commitment, veteran ownership, team, and operating principles. Keep it human and specific.

#### `/resources`

High-intent practical answers such as where to recycle a computer locally, what to do with a hard drive, whether old servers retain value, or how a business should prepare retired devices.

#### Local pages

Create city pages only when each page can contain genuinely useful city-specific information. Do not mass-produce near-duplicate SEO pages.

## Motion system

Motion has four allowed jobs:

1. **Orient** — show which path the visitor chose.
2. **Explain** — reveal how an item or asset moves through EVER's process.
3. **Confirm** — acknowledge a selection, completed step, or submitted request.
4. **Transition** — carry one narrative idea into the next scene.

If an animation does not do one of those jobs, remove it.

### Motion vocabulary

- **Route draw:** a line advances from origin to outcome.
- **Sort:** an equipment tile moves into an accepted, special handling, or business pathway.
- **Scan:** a subtle cyan sweep reveals useful metadata once, never continuously.
- **Resolve:** several inputs become one concise pickup summary.
- **Handoff:** the active accent moves from one narrative scene to the next.

This vocabulary is derived from the real service, making the motion recognizable as EVER rather than a purchased animation library.

### Timing

- Hover/focus response: 120–180ms
- Button and selection state: 180–240ms
- Result transition: 250–400ms
- Section-level route or reveal: 500–800ms
- One-time hero sequence: no more than 1200ms

Use a confident ease-out for entering content and a faster ease-in for exits. Avoid springy overshoot, elastic movement, and dramatic 3D rotation.

### Motion budget

At any moment, there should be no more than:

- One primary narrative animation
- One local response caused by direct user input
- One subtle ambient effect, if the scene truly needs it

Most of the page should be still most of the time. No motion should loop indefinitely except an essential system progress indicator.

## Scroll behavior

Use scroll to reveal relationships, not to make the visitor wait.

- Hero content is immediately readable before animation completes.
- The hero route may advance slightly with the first scroll, then stops.
- The commercial navy field should enter as a clean color transition, not a rotating page or curtain gimmick.
- The process section may use a short sticky stage on wide screens, limited to roughly one viewport of additional travel.
- Content reveals in semantic groups, not word by word.
- Section headlines may shift 12–24px as they settle; avoid giant parallax gaps.
- Images can move at a subtly different rate only when it creates depth and does not crop important content.
- Do not hijack scroll, change scroll speed, or trap visitors inside a sequence.

Each scene should still make sense when animations are disabled or when a user jumps to it from a link.

## Interaction patterns and microinteractions

### Buttons

Primary buttons use navy or blue with a crisp directional cue. On hover or focus, the internal route line advances a few pixels and the arrow follows. The button itself should not float, glow, or chase the pointer.

### Equipment choices

Use recognizable item names and illustrations. Hover can reveal a single useful detail; selection changes border, fill, icon, and text so state is not color-only.

### Forms

- Ask one coherent group of questions at a time.
- Keep visible labels above inputs.
- Explain why information is needed.
- Use helpful defaults only when they are honest and reversible.
- Show a persistent plain-language summary on desktop and a collapsible summary on mobile.
- Validate near the field and preserve all valid entries after an error.
- Never use a fake conversational typing effect.

### Upload

When an equipment list is uploaded, show the real filename, file type, size, removal control, and upload state. A brief scan-line may accompany processing, but do not pretend the site has parsed or valued equipment unless it actually has.

### Confirmation

After a business request, replace the form with a clear receipt: what was submitted, what happens next, expected response window if supportable, and how to make a correction. A single route completes with a static check. No confetti.

### Navigation

The header is transparent or white over the bright hero, then gains a solid white surface and thin divider after scrolling. It should not constantly shrink and expand. On the navy commercial scene it may invert once, with sufficient contrast.

## Mobile behavior

Mobile is not a compressed desktop animation.

- Put the core answer and actions above visual spectacle.
- Stack the two hero paths as large full-width controls.
- Replace hover-dependent information with tap or always-visible text.
- Convert the process path to a vertical flow without scroll pinning.
- Keep filters and acceptance results within thumb reach.
- Use a persistent bottom action bar only on high-intent pages: `Drop-off info` and `Business pickup`. It should not cover content or appear on every article.
- Use native-feeling inputs and allow camera-based photo attachment only if operations can use those photos.
- Collapse the pickup summary into a clearly labeled sheet; do not obscure form progress.
- Keep at least 44px targets, preferably 48–56px for primary choices.
- Avoid large autoplay media and effects that compete with device performance or cellular data.

On mobile, the personality comes from typography, route geometry, color transitions, and decisive state changes—not from reduced versions of desktop parallax.

## Accessibility guardrails

- Meet WCAG 2.2 AA contrast for text, controls, focus, and meaningful graphics.
- Provide full keyboard operation for selectors, filters, forms, navigation, and dialogs.
- Use strong, consistent focus indicators that fit the blue/cyan visual language.
- Keep source order meaningful; animated visual order must not contradict reading order.
- Do not rely on color, position, or motion alone to communicate acceptance or completion.
- Provide descriptive text alternatives for informative images and empty alternatives for decoration.
- Use semantic buttons, headings, lists, status messages, and form labels.
- Announce dynamic acceptance results and form errors without moving focus unexpectedly.
- Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`; remove route drawing, parallax, smooth scrolling, and auto-advancing behavior while preserving immediate final states.
- Pause any nonessential animation when the page is hidden.
- Avoid flashing, rapid contrast changes, and moving patterns behind text.
- Maintain readable type at 200% zoom and support reflow at narrow widths.

## Performance guardrails

- The headline, choices, and first useful answer must render without waiting for animation code.
- Prefer CSS transforms and opacity for simple transitions; use a motion library only for coordinated state that genuinely needs it.
- Use SVG for route geometry and brand-line illustrations, keeping path complexity low.
- Do not ship a full 3D/WebGL scene for the hero.
- Reserve image dimensions to prevent layout shift.
- Use responsive modern image formats and lazy-load below-the-fold photography.
- Avoid autoplay background video. If later used for a real operational story, provide a poster, no-audio default, reduced-data alternative, and manual control.
- Load only the font weights used and provide durable system fallbacks.
- Target a fast mobile Largest Contentful Paint, near-zero layout shift, and responsive interaction under real mid-range phone conditions—not only a designer's workstation.
- Test motion with CPU throttling and on a low-power mobile device. If an effect drops frames, simplify it rather than hiding it behind a loader.

## Copy voice

EVER speaks like a capable operator: short, specific, calm, and useful.

Good direction:

- “Old tech? We'll take it.”
- “Can I bring this?”
- “Your old technology still has a path forward.”
- “We don't treat usable technology like trash.”
- “Tell us what you have.”
- “We'll tell you the next step.”
- “Pickup, inventory, recovery, and responsible recycling.”

Avoid:

- “Join us on our mission to save the planet.”
- “Innovative eco-solutions for a greener tomorrow.”
- “Unlock the power of circularity.”
- “Seamless, sustainable, end-to-end solutions.”
- Claims such as “100% secure,” “zero landfill,” “certified,” or “highest value” without support.

## What specifically to avoid

### Generic AI recycling-site signals

- A smiling stock family holding an old laptop
- A blue-green gradient covering every surface
- Leaf, globe, recycle-arrow, and circuit icons used together
- Floating glass cards over a hero image
- Three identical icon cards labeled “Recycle, Reuse, Renew”
- A looping animated globe or particle network
- Vague copy about “a sustainable future” with no operational answer
- Invented impact counters and social proof
- A generic chatbot as the primary intake experience
- Perfectly repeated split-screen sections down the whole page
- Excessive rounded rectangles, pill labels, shadows, and gradients
- Neon glows, blurred orbs, and fake holographic interfaces
- Decorative dashboard panels with imaginary inventory data
- Random serial numbers, scanning text, or command-line decoration
- Fake “live pickup” indicators, map vehicles, or notification toasts
- Animating every headline by individual letter
- Scroll-jacking, horizontal-scroll galleries, or long pinned sequences

### Brand dilution

- Turning lime into the primary site color
- Placing the veteran-owned badge inside the main logo
- Using flags, stars, camouflage, or military styling as a recurring motif
- Redrawing or distorting the `V`
- Making the circuit pattern a full-page background
- Mixing multiple icon styles or using emoji as production icons
- Making residential drop-off look secondary to the aspirational ITAD story
- Making the business offer sound more mature or certified than operations are

### Operational friction

- Hiding fees or acceptance restrictions behind a contact form
- Asking residents for personal information before answering a basic question
- A single generic quote form for both residents and businesses
- Forcing businesses to explain an inventory in an open text box
- Publishing hours, locations, turnaround times, or service areas that are not maintained as structured source-of-truth data
- Promising automated valuation, tracking, certificates, or reporting before those systems exist

## The distinct design signature

EVER's recognizable system should be built from five things:

1. The white field and deep navy editorial type
2. The approved blue–cyan–lime `V`
3. A route that branches, sorts, and resolves according to the real service
4. Authentic close-up technology and operations photography
5. Immediate tools that answer “Can I bring this?” and “Can you pick this up?”

These elements are specific enough to be ownable and restrained enough to age well. The site should remain compelling in a screenshot with all animation paused. Motion then makes the system clearer—it does not rescue the design.

## Build and review sequence

Develop the experience one approved scene at a time:

1. Finalize production logo assets, exact color tokens, typeface, service facts, and content source of truth.
2. Prototype the hero routing junction and verify that it is effective with motion disabled.
3. Build the residential acceptance finder against maintained service data.
4. Build the commercial pickup brief and confirmation flow.
5. Add the EVER process narrative using authentic operational details.
6. Add value recovery, local coverage, trust, and resource content only as facts are available.
7. Test keyboard, screen reader, reduced-motion, low-power mobile, slow network, error, and empty states.
8. Validate every public claim and remove all placeholder proof before launch.

The standard for each scene is simple: it must clarify the service, increase trust, or help the visitor act. If it does none of those, it does not belong on the page.
